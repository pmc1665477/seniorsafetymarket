Why Lift Chairs Are Rated So Highly by Seniors — and How to Pick the Right One

By the Senior Safety Market team · Published Oct 2026 · Updated Oct 2026

Lift chairs consistently rank among the highest-rated mobility aids for seniors living alone — not because of massage settings or heated seats, but because of one simple thing: they let someone stand up safely, by themselves, without waiting for help. Here's what actually separates a good lift chair from a mediocre one, and what to check before you buy.

Three-Position vs. Infinite-Position: What's the Real Difference

Most lift chairs fall into one of two categories, and the difference matters more than the price tag suggests.

  • Three-position chairs use a single motor that moves the back and footrest together through three stops: upright, reclined, and lifted. They're simpler, more affordable, and have fewer parts to break — a solid choice for someone who mainly needs help standing up and sitting down.
  • Infinite-position chairs use multiple motors so the backrest and leg rest move independently, letting the chair stop at virtually any angle — including a zero-gravity position with the legs raised above the heart. These cost more but function much more like a genuine recliner the rest of the time, not just a "lift" chair.

If the chair will be used for hours of daily sitting — not just the lift function — the extra cost of an infinite-position model is usually worth it. If it's mainly there for safe transfers, three-position is often all that's needed.

Why Independence Is the Real Selling Point

Lift chairs are classified by the FDA as Class II medical devices because of the lifting mechanism itself — which means that when a doctor documents medical necessity, the lift mechanism's cost may be partially reimbursable through Medicare or private insurance, even though the chair itself (the furniture) typically isn't covered. That distinction trips a lot of people up, so it's worth confirming directly with your provider before assuming what will or won't be paid for.

The bigger point, though, is independence: a good lift chair is built so the person using it can operate it and transfer in and out on their own — which matters enormously for someone living alone or without a caregiver nearby, and can also make it easier to transfer safely to a walker or mobility scooter afterward.

As with any mobility equipment, buy a margin above the user's actual weight and body size rather than cutting it close — and check the seat width and depth, not just the weight rating, since a chair that's technically rated high enough can still feel cramped for a larger-framed person.

What to Check Before You Buy (New or Used)

  • Test the lift function fully extended — it should rise smoothly without jerking, shaking, or stalling partway up.
  • Check the remote control — buttons should respond immediately, and on infinite-position models, confirm the back and leg rest actually move independently as advertised.
  • Sit in it at full recline — make sure the chair stays stable and doesn't feel like it could tip, especially on carpet versus hard flooring.
  • Ask about battery backup — many infinite-position models include a battery backup so the chair can still lower during a power outage; this is worth confirming, not assuming.
  • Inspect the upholstery and frame — on a used chair, check for sagging in the seat cushion and listen for grinding or straining sounds from the motor during the lift cycle.
Power outage safety: If the chair doesn't have battery backup, make sure the household has a plan for getting the user safely out of a fully reclined position if the power goes out — this is an easy detail to overlook until it actually happens.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Medicare cover a lift chair?

Medicare may cover a portion of the lifting mechanism's cost as durable medical equipment when a doctor documents medical necessity — but typically not the chair itself as furniture. Confirm current requirements directly with your provider or Medicare before assuming coverage.

Is an infinite-position lift chair worth the extra cost?

If the chair will be used for hours of everyday sitting and reclining, yes — the independent back and leg movement functions much more like a real recliner. If the chair is mainly needed for safe standing and sitting, a three-position chair is often enough.

What happens to a lift chair during a power outage?

Chairs without a battery backup won't operate the lift or recline functions until power returns, which is why it's worth asking about battery backup availability and having a plan in place regardless.

What weight capacity should I look for?

Choose a chair rated meaningfully above the user's current weight, and check seat width and depth in addition to the weight rating — a technically-rated-high-enough chair can still feel too narrow for a larger frame.
