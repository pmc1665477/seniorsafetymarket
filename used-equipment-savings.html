Used Senior Care Equipment: How Much You Can Really Save

By the Senior Safety Market team · Published Jul 2026 · Updated Aug 2026

Equipment needs for an aging parent or loved one usually don't come with much warning — a fall, a hospital discharge, a new diagnosis — and they land at exactly the moment a family has the least time to shop around and the most other costs already piling up. Here's what senior care equipment actually costs new, what Medicare will and won't pay for, and how much buying used can realistically put back in a family's pocket.

What Medicare Actually Covers (and What It Doesn't)

Medicare Part B typically covers 80% of the cost of durable medical equipment — wheelchairs, hospital beds, walkers — when a doctor documents medical necessity and it's supplied through a Medicare-approved provider. But that still leaves the remaining 20% plus the annual Part B deductible as an out-of-pocket cost, and it usually only covers a basic, rental-grade version of the equipment — not the lighter, more comfortable, or more full-featured model a family might actually want.

That remaining 20% — plus anything beyond the basic covered model — is exactly where buying used equipment directly from another family or caregiver tends to make the biggest difference.

Add up even a modest list — a wheelchair or scooter, a hospital bed, a lift chair — and a family can easily be facing $2,500-$8,000 in equipment costs after Medicare's share, right at a moment when they're also absorbing new caregiving time, medical bills, and possibly lost income. Buying that same list used typically runs 40-65% less, since most of this equipment is sold not because it wore out, but because the previous owner no longer needs it — often the most bittersweet reason a "gently used" listing exists at all.

Why the savings matter more here than in most other used-goods categories: equipment needs for a senior often show up suddenly and all at once, not spread out over years like most purchases. Saving 40-65% on several pieces of equipment in the same week can be the difference between affording it right away and delaying care a family actually needs now.

What to Check Before Buying Used

  • Test every motor and function in person — wheels, recline, height adjustment, battery charge on power equipment — before you buy, not after you get it home.
  • Ask about hours of use, not just age — a 5-year-old chair used occasionally can be in better shape than a 1-year-old one used daily.
  • Check weight capacity against the actual user's weight, with a safety margin — this matters most for wheelchairs, scooters, and hospital beds.
  • Inspect upholstery and padding for tears or deep staining — replacement cushions and covers are a normal, inexpensive fix and still leave the total well under buying new.
  • For anything electric or battery-powered, ask when the battery was last replaced — it's the component most likely to need replacing soonest on a used power wheelchair or scooter.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is used senior care equipment safe to buy?

Yes, generally — as long as you check weight capacity, test motors and functions in person, and inspect the frame and upholstery as described above. The components most likely to need attention are batteries on power equipment and worn padding, both inexpensive to address.

Does Medicare cover the full cost of equipment like wheelchairs and hospital beds?

Usually not entirely. Medicare Part B typically covers 80% of a medically necessary item through an approved supplier, leaving the remaining 20% plus the deductible to the family — and often only for a basic model, not necessarily the one that best fits the person's needs.

How much can buying used realistically save?

Typically 40-65% off new pricing, depending on the item and its condition. On a multi-item list (a mobility device plus a hospital bed or lift chair, for example), that can mean thousands of dollars in savings at exactly the time a family needs it most.

Why is so much senior care equipment sold gently used?

Most listings exist because the previous owner's needs changed — they recovered, moved to a facility that provides equipment, or passed away — not because the equipment wore out. That's a large part of why quality used equipment is so widely available at a steep discount from new.
